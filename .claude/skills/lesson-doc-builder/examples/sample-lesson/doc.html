Every one of them started in the same place: years of lessons, a streak on an app, and a mind that went blank the moment a real person asked a question. The fix wasn't more vocabulary. It was retrieval.

More input feels like progress. It isn't.

Most learners keep adding. Another podcast, another word list, another series with subtitles. It feels like it's all going in.

What actually happens is you build a huge library you can't find anything in. You recognise almost every word. You can produce almost none of them.

You don't have a knowledge problem. You have a door problem. The words are in the room. The door only opens one way.

The fewer words you practise pulling out, the more of them you can actually use.

The Speaking Loop

You can't control how fast someone talks to you. But you can decide what you practise pulling out, how often, and in front of whom. That's the Speaking Loop, and it has three parts.

Map The Three Parts

Small Set (what) + Daily Recall (how) + Live Reps (where) = words that come out when you need them

This isn't just a diagram. I've turned it into a free one-page planner, the Speaking Loop Planner, so you can map your own as we go. I'll show you exactly how to use it in a minute.

Each part has one job. The set keeps it small enough to finish. The recall opens the door. The live reps prove it works under pressure. Miss one and the whole thing stalls.

Build It In Order, Then Test It

  1. Choose the thirty sentences you'd actually say this week.
  2. Recall them out loud, from a prompt in your own language, every day.
  3. Use five of them with a real person before the week ends.

Skip the first step and the other two are guesses.

Listening fills the room. Speaking is practising the door.

Part #1: What Would You Actually Say This Week?

Most word lists are organised by topic. "Food. Travel. Family." That tells you nothing about what will come out of your own mouth on Thursday.

A Small Set is thirty full sentences from your real week, written the way you'd say them.

Build It From Three Places

1. What You Said Today

The things you said in your own language that you'd want to say in Spanish.

2. What You Got Stuck On

The sentence you started last time and couldn't finish.

3. What Comes Next

The one conversation you know is coming this week.

Here's the difference a real sentence makes. A word list gives you "appointment". Your week gives you "Can I move my appointment to Friday morning?"

The first one you'll recognise. The second one you'll say.

A word is something you know. A sentence is something you can use.

Diagnose Your Set

  • You never finish reviewing it: it's too big. Thirty, not three hundred.
  • You know every item but still freeze: it's single words. Rewrite them as sentences you'd say.
  • It bores you: it came from a textbook, not your week. Start from what you said today.

Once you know what you're practising, the next question is how to make it come out on demand.

Part #2: Where Does Speaking Speed Actually Come From?

Here's the part most advice gets wrong. Reading a sentence again doesn't make it faster to say. Pulling it out of your head does. The problem was never how much you studied. It was which direction you studied in.

Daily Recall means you see the prompt in your own language and say the Spanish out loud before you look.

It feels worse. You'll get half of them wrong on day one. That struggle is the door opening.

Give Each Direction Its Share

Roughly five in every ten minutes should be recall out loud. The rest is input: listening and reading that feeds next week's set.

Diagnose Your Recall

  • You get them all right first time: you're peeking, or the set is stale. Cover the answer.
  • You do it in your head: say it out loud. Your mouth needs the reps, not your eyes.
  • You skip days: ten minutes is too long for you right now. Do four.

Now there's one part left, and it's the one everybody avoids.

Part #3: Who Will You Say It To?

A sentence you've only ever said to your bedroom wall isn't finished. Live Reps are the five sentences from your set you use with a real person this week.

Keep it to one line: "This week I'll say ▁▁▁▁▁ to ▁▁▁▁▁ by ▁▁▁▁▁."

Diagnose Your Reps

  • You have nobody to speak to: a voice note to a tutor counts. A voice note to yourself doesn't.
  • You wait until you're ready: ready is what the rep produces, not what it needs.
  • You plan ten and do none: plan five. Put a name next to each one.
Recall makes it possible. Live reps make it automatic.

If You're Learning For Work

Take someone who has a weekly call with a team in Madrid.

  • Small Set: the thirty sentences that call always needs. Updates, questions, "can you repeat that?"
  • Daily Recall: ten minutes before the first meeting of the day.
  • Live Reps: five sentences, on that call, this week.

If You're Learning For Family Or Travel

Take someone visiting their partner's parents next month.

  • Small Set: the dinner table. Compliments, stories, offers to help.
  • Daily Recall: on the walk to work, out loud, under your breath.
  • Live Reps: a voice note to your partner every other day.
Don't start with "how many words do I know?" Start with "what will I say on Thursday?"

Two Honest Limitations

  • This doesn't replace input. The set gets smaller, but you still listen and read. That's where next week's sentences come from.
  • Your first set will be slightly wrong. You'll pick sentences you never use. Swap them out after each real conversation.

Test It On Your Last Ten Study Sessions

Look back at your last ten sessions and tag each one: Small Set, Daily Recall, Live Reps, or none of them.

Anything tagged "none" was input. Useful, but it isn't what makes you speak.

Thirty sentences. Ten minutes. Five real conversations.

That's the Speaking Loop: a Small Set from your real week, Daily Recall that opens the door, and Live Reps that prove it under pressure.

You won't suddenly be fluent. But you stop collecting words you can't use, and start saying the ones you need.
